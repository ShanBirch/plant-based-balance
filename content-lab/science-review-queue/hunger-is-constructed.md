## Script

Your hunger isn't a little fuel gauge that simply clicks on when your stomach's empty.

It's something your brain helps construct.

Your body sends real signals from your gut, hormones, and available energy. But those signals don't arrive with a neat label saying, "you're hungry."

Your brain combines them with the time of day, what you can see and smell, and what it's learned before. Then it creates the feeling you recognise as hunger.

And this paper gets fucking weird.

Crum and colleagues gave 46 people the same 380-calorie milkshake twice. One label called it a 620-calorie indulgent shake. The other called it a 140-calorie sensible shake.

Same drink. Different expectation.

When people believed it was indulgent, their ghrelin fell much more steeply, even though the nutrition was identical.

Before someone tries to manifest themselves into a calorie deficit, no. Thoughts don't override energy balance. This was a small study measuring a short-term hormone response, not fat loss.

But it does show that hunger is biological and contextual. You're not weak. Your brain is trying to interpret messy data.

## Science Notes

### Primary paper

- Title: *Mind over milkshakes: Mindsets, not just nutrients, determine ghrelin response*
- Authors: Alia J. Crum, William R. Corbin, Kelly D. Brownell, and Peter Salovey
- Journal: *Health Psychology*
- Year: 2011
- Study type: Within-participant human experiment
- Sample: 46 adults
- DOI: `10.1037/a0023467`
- PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21574706/
- What it supports: Beliefs about the same drink altered the short-term ghrelin response.
- What it does not support: Thoughts cancelling calories, expectations causing fat loss, or every experience of hunger being a mistaken prediction.

### Conceptual support

- Title: *Interoceptive inference and prediction in food-related disorders*
- Authors: Madhav Subramanian and Christoph A. Thaiss
- Journal: *Genes & Development*
- Year: 2024
- Study type: Outlook/review, not an intervention trial
- DOI: `10.1101/gad.352301.124`
- PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11535158/
- Relevance: Describes feeding as an integration of internal body signals, external sensory cues, learned expectations, and anticipatory responses.
